Milling picks: the carbide, the physics, and how to check either of us

Two suppliers, two data sheets, one 6% cobalt tungsten carbide on each. This report works out what those sheets imply, shows every equation it uses, and ends with six measurements that settle the question without trusting anybody.

Every figure below is live: move a control and the microstructure, the charts and the numbers all recompute from the same model. Nothing is a picture of a result.

03 — The first performance indicator

Rotation is what makes the tip last

A milling pick that turns in its holder wears into a symmetric cone and keeps a point. A pick that stops turning wears flat on one face, loses its point, and then loads the holder instead of cutting. On asphalt this is the single largest difference between a pick that runs a shift and one that does not.

The rotation is not incidental. The holder sets the pick axis at an angle to the cutting direction, so the material meets one side of the cone first. That asymmetric contact produces a moment about the pick's own axis and indexes it a few degrees per cut. The angle is a property of the drum and holder — not of the pick. The pick's only job is to not resist the turn.

A teaching model, not a measurement: a rigid-body moment balance about the pick axis with assumed friction coefficients (0.11 coated, 0.20 oxide; ×2.2 for debris ingress, ×1.7 for a deformed sleeve), tip contact radius 9.5 mm and shank radius 9 mm. It shows which way each variable pushes and roughly how hard — it does not predict a specific pick on a specific drum.

Why a tip that is not centred costs you rotation

When the carbide is brazed off the centreline of the steel body, the contact point migrates toward the axis on one half of every revolution, so the indexing moment falls and alternates. Three things follow, and all three are visible in the field: the tooth runs out of balance and vibrates, one flank takes a deeper cut than the other and wears first, and the misalignment feeds bending stress into the body and the holder. An offset tip is a rotation problem before it is a wear problem — which is why we set concentricity at the braze fixture rather than inspect for it afterwards.

04 — The microstructure

Seeing the difference the grain size makes

Tungsten carbide is not a solid. It is a skeleton of hard WC grains cemented by a tough cobalt binder — grey grains, amber binder below. Both panels hold the same 6% cobalt by weight, so the amber area is identical in both. Only the grain size changes. This is the whole argument in one picture.

It is drawn to scale and to the correct binder fraction, so the geometry is right; it is not an image of any particular lot. A real ×1500 micrograph of the lot supplied ships with the certificate.

Coarse grain — softer, tougher

Longer cobalt ligaments between grains can stretch and absorb energy before a crack runs through them. The material abrades faster in a lab test and survives impact and heat cycling in the field. This is the milling-pick regime.

Fine grain — harder, more brittle

Grains packed tightly leave the cobalt short, disconnected runs. There is less room for the binder to stretch, so the material resists abrasion well and cracks easily. Good for finishing, nozzles, wear parts. On a milling drum it chips.

Uniformity matters as much as the average. If grain sizes vary widely, some regions are hard and brittle while others are soft and wear quickly; the boundary between them is where a crack starts. That is a sintering-control question, and it is section 11.

05 — The model, in full

The seven equations this report runs on

Nothing here is proprietary and nothing is hidden. These are the standard relations for cemented carbide, with the constants fitted to our own measured grade sheet. Live values update with the estimator in section 06.

1 — Weight percent is not volume percent

VCo = wCo / ρCowCo / ρCo + wWC / ρWC    ρWC = 15.63, ρCo = 8.90 g/cm³

Cobalt is 43% lighter than tungsten carbide, so 6% by weight is about 10% by volume. The microstructure responds to volume, never to weight — which is why a grade sheet quoting only weight percent is telling you less than it appears to.

—

2 — Contiguity: how much grain touches grain

CWC = 0.75 − 1.8 · VCo   (bounded 0.15 … 0.80)

The fraction of each grain's surface in contact with another grain rather than with binder. The carbide forms a continuous load-bearing skeleton; contiguity says how well-connected it is. Fitted to Gurland's measurements on WC–Co.

—

3 — Fullman's relation: the binder mean free path

λ = d · VCo(1 − VCo) · (1 − CWC)

The average uninterrupted run through cobalt between two carbide grains. This — not the grain size itself — is the length scale that sets almost everything. Two grades with different grain sizes and different cobalt contents but the same λ behave alike.

—

4 — Hall–Petch on the binder path: hardness

HV = HV0 + k · λ−1/2 − m · (VCo − Vref)   HV0 = 999, k = 331, m = 810

The carbide grains barely deform; the cobalt does. Plastic flow happens by dislocations running through binder pools and piling up at the carbide interfaces. A shorter free run means a shorter pile-up, which means more stress to keep it moving — the classic λ−1/2 form. The constants are set so that 6% cobalt at 5 µm reproduces the 87.6–88.4 HRA measured on our sheet.

—

5 — Fracture toughness

KIc = K0 + a · λ1/2 + b · VCo   K0 = 4.96, a = 4.78, b = 21.0

A crack running through carbide is arrested when it meets cobalt, which stretches plastically behind the crack tip and pulls the faces back together. A longer ligament absorbs more energy before it snaps, so toughness rises with λ exactly where hardness falls. This single fact is why you cannot have both.

—

6 — Critical flaw size: why toughness counts twice

ac = 1π · ( KIcY · σ )2

Griffith. The largest defect a tip can carry before a crack runs scales with the square of toughness. A 20% loss of KIc is a 36% loss in the size of pore, braze void or coarse grain the tip can survive. This is the term that decides chipping on impact, and it is why the life model in section 08 weights toughness quadratically.

—

7 — Coercivity: the binder path, measured in thirty seconds

Hc ≈ A · λ−0.8   A = 90 (Oe · µm0.8)

Cobalt is ferromagnetic; tungsten carbide is not. Magnetic domain walls moving through the binder are pinned by the same carbide interfaces that pin dislocations, so the field needed to demagnetise the part is set by the same λ. A magnetometer therefore reads the microstructure directly — non-destructively, in under a minute, on the finished part. This is the single most useful number a carbide supplier can give you, and it is the one AOJI does not publish.

—

Density is the inverse rule of mixtures, ρ = 100 / (wCo/ρCo + wWC/ρWC), which gives 14.95 g/cm³ for a 94/6 grade. Transverse rupture strength is modelled as TRS = 2400 · (1 + 0.045(w−6)) · (2/d)0.18 and always shown as a band, because TRS depends more on sintering, hot isostatic pressing and carbon balance than on composition. See section 15 for what each constant is fitted to.

08 — The answer to the actual question

Which grain size wins on a milling drum

The abrasion index says finer is better. It is measuring the wrong thing. A milling pick on asphalt does not fail by polishing away in clean abrasion — it fails by chipping on joints, manhole rings, aggregate and reinforcement, and by thermal cycling between the cut and the air. The right question is not "which is harder" but "what fraction of this duty is impact?"

Duty-weighted service index

L = 100 · ( A / 100 )1−β · ( I / 100 )β    A = 100 (HV/HVref)2.5,  I = 100 (KIc/Kref)2

A geometric weighting of the abrasion index A and the impact index I, with β the impact share of the duty. I is quadratic in KIc because that is how critical flaw size scales (equation 6). β is an engineering judgement, not a measurement — it is the one input here nobody can derive from a data sheet, and it is exactly what the field trial in section 14 is designed to pin down.

The cross-over sits near an impact share of 0.49. Below it the harder, finer grade wins on life; above it the coarse grade does, and by a widening margin. Asphalt planing is not a close call — it sits well above the line. That is the entire reason our milling tip is specified at 4.0–6.0 µm and not at 1.2–2.0 µm, and it is why a pick chosen on hardness alone comes off the drum early.

Where this model stops

The fit is valid over 0.4–8 µm. It must not be read as "coarser is always better". Past roughly 8 µm, abnormal grain growth, falling transverse rupture strength and edge chipping reverse the trend, and the curve above has no term for any of them. 4.0–6.0 µm is a chosen balance, not the end of a slope.

Density is quoted as a catalogue range

The verifying measurements are absent

Taken together, the sheet reads as a catalogue page rather than a production certificate, and its numbers do not describe one consistent material. 10 — The measurement that settles it

Coercivity separates the two claims in under a minute

Hardness testing needs a prepared flat and reads the surface. Grain size needs a polished, etched section and a microscope. Coercivity needs neither. It is a non-destructive magnetic measurement on the finished tip, it takes well under a minute, and by equation 7 it reads the binder mean free path directly — which is to say, it reads the grain size.

The two claims are not close on this axis. They are far apart, and the gap is far larger than any measurement uncertainty:

Magnetic saturation is the natural companion measurement: it scales with the amount of free cobalt, so it reads the carbon balance. Low saturation means carbon-deficient eta phase, which is brittle; high saturation means free graphite, which is porous and weak. Coercivity and saturation together bracket both structure and chemistry, and neither requires cutting up a tip.

11 — Where grain size is actually decided

Grain size is not chosen in the powder. It is chosen in the furnace.

Sintering runs in four stages — powder preparation, blending, compacting, sintering. Tungsten, cobalt and carbon powders are brought to size and purity, blended to a homogeneous mixture, pressed to shape, then heated in vacuum or controlled atmosphere until the cobalt melts and the compact densifies. During that liquid phase, small WC grains dissolve and re-deposit on large ones. The grains grow, and how much they grow is set by temperature and time:

8 — Ostwald ripening during liquid-phase sintering

d³ − d0³ = K0 · exp( −Q / RT ) · t   Q ≈ 400 kJ/mol

Growth is cubic in time and exponential in temperature. The exponential is the problem: because Q/RT is around 28 at sintering temperature, a 2% error in absolute temperature changes the growth rate by roughly 60%. Powder specification does not protect you from this. Furnace control does.

Q = 400 kJ/mol is a mid-range literature value for WC coarsening in liquid cobalt; K0 is back-fitted so that the nominal schedule — 1410 °C for 60 minutes from 1.2 µm powder — lands at 5.0 µm. The purpose of this figure is the sensitivity, not the absolute calibration of any particular furnace.

Why we sinter in vacuum

Purity

The vacuum removes oxygen before it can oxidise the powder surface. Oxide films block the cobalt from wetting the carbide, and what does not wet does not densify.

Density and porosity

Gas trapped between particles has nowhere to go once the surface closes. Evacuating first is what makes an A02 B00 C00 porosity rating reachable at all.

Grain control

Precise, uniform temperature is the only lever on the exponential above. A furnace that holds ±10 °C across the load holds the grade; one that holds ±60 °C does not.

This is also the honest answer to why a certificate matters more than a sample. Sample quantities are made with care, often from the best material on the floor. The risk is the fifth production lot eighteen months later, when the same part number arrives from a furnace that has drifted — and nobody notices until the drum comes off the job early. Coercivity, magnetic saturation and porosity catch that drift because they are quick, non-destructive and repeatable. A supplier who measures them per lot can prove the fifth lot matches the first. A supplier who publishes a catalogue page cannot, and neither can you.

12 — Beyond the tip

The same trade-off, decided twice

The carbide decides most of the life, but not all of it. The steel body faces the identical problem from section 04 — hard wears well, hard breaks — and solves it a different way. Instead of picking one grain size for the whole part, induction hardening puts fine, hard structure at the surface and leaves coarse, tough structure in the core.

Hardness against depth below the surface. A hardened case resists body wash-out and holder wear; the untransformed core keeps the bending toughness that stops the shank snapping.

Why medium-frequency induction rather than a furnace

A furnace heats the whole part and the whole part transforms. Induction drives eddy currents into the surface layer only, so the case forms where wear happens and the core is left alone. It is also fast, which matters more than it sounds: short cycles mean less time at temperature, less scaling and oxidation, and less opportunity for the result to drift between the first part and the thousandth. Precise, repeatable, selective — the same three words that matter in the carbide furnace.

The three remaining components

Proprietary shank coating

A water-based zinc–aluminium flake coating rather than a black-oxide conversion layer. It does two things: it resists corrosion far better in humid and coastal service, and it leaves a smoother bore so sand and rust cannot embed and seize the shank. Section 03 shows what that friction difference does to rotation. Black oxide is a thin conversion layer — effective to a degree, but not the same protection and not the same surface.

Wear plate, 48 mm forged

Larger than most standard plates, which is the point: more of the tool holder is covered and protected. Forged from a single piece rather than cast, so the grain flow follows the shape, porosity is low and there is no joint to start a crack. Forging also holds the taper between body and plate consistent, and that taper is what centres the pick in the holder.

Sleeve

Its whole job is to hold a clearance. A sleeve that deforms under load closes that clearance, and a pick that cannot turn is a pick that wears on one face. Same alloy and same heat treatment as the body, chosen for resistance to permanent deformation rather than for outright hardness.

What decides these in service is body wash-out, tip retention and whether the pick rotates — all three measurable on site rather than arguable in a document. They are part of what we propose to measure in section 14.

Hardness uses a Hall–Petch fit on binder mean free path, calibrated so that 6% cobalt at 5 µm reproduces the 87.6–88.4 HRA measured on the EVERPADS grade sheet; mean free path comes from Fullman's relation with contiguity estimated from cobalt volume. Fracture toughness and coercivity are fitted to published WC–Co ranges, with coercivity anchored to the 66–80 Oe on the same sheet. Transverse rupture strength depends heavily on sintering, hot isostatic pressing and carbon control, so it is shown as a band rather than a number. The abrasion and service indices are relative and take the EVERPADS tip as 100; they indicate direction, not tooth life. Figures described as estimates are calculated, not measured. Everything presented as a specification comes from one of the two grade sheets and can be checked against the certificates requested in section 13.
